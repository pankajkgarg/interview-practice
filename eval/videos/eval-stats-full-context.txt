[0:37] 1. A score is an estimate (standard error and confidence intervals)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter one. A score is an estimate.
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this every time someone reads a single number off a dashboard: a checkpoint's
score, a pass rate on one category, a judge's average.
    (On screen: three small dashboard tiles: 'GSM8K 72.0%', 'Code: 41.5%', 'Judge mean
    6.6')
Here is today's version. A researcher writes:
    (On screen: chat bubble appears)
"New checkpoint scores 72.0 percent on our 500-question reasoning benchmark. Last week's
scored 70.4. Did the data change work?"
    (On screen: bubble text types out; whiteboard shows 70.4% -> 72.0%, n = 500)
That is a 1.6 point improvement. The question is whether it is real.

THINK
Pause and think. What would you tell them, and what would you need to know first?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting answer: 72 is bigger than 70.4, so yes, it helped. Ship the data change.
    (On screen: green tick next to 72.0)
But that treats 72.0 as an exact property of the model, like its parameter count. Is it?
    (On screen: tick fades into a question mark)

OBSERVATION
Here is the question that unlocks it. If someone had written a different 500 questions
of the same kind, would the model still score exactly 72.0?
Let's find out. Take a model whose true accuracy is 71 percent. Give it a fresh set of
500 questions. It scores one number.
    (On screen: one dot drops onto an x-axis from 63% to 78%)
Another fresh set. A different number. And again.
    (On screen: a few more dots drop, each landing somewhere different)
Now two thousand reruns, same model every time.
    (On screen: fast-forward: dots pile into the histogram (pilot data reruns_hist))
The same model scored anywhere from 63.2 to 77.4 percent. About 7 in 10 of the reruns
landed within two points of the truth.
    (On screen: bracket the central plus or minus 2 points, label 71%)
So both of the researcher's numbers, 70.4 and 72.0, sit comfortably inside the noise of
a single model. The score is an estimate, and it wobbles.
    (On screen: markers at 70.4 and 72.0 on the histogram, both inside the bracket)

TOOL
How big is the wobble, without two thousand reruns? Start with where the probability
comes from.
Think of each question as a coin. It lands 'correct' with some probability p, the
model's true accuracy, which we cannot see.
    (On screen: a row of 500 coins; each flips to a green tick or red cross)
Our best guess for p is simply the score. 360 correct out of 500 is 0.72. We call it
p-hat. The probability in the formula is the eval score itself.
    (On screen: counter 360 / 500 = 0.72, label 'p-hat = the score')
One coin with probability 0.72 has variance p times one minus p: 0.72 times 0.28, which
is 0.20.
    (On screen: formula panel line 1: p(1-p) = 0.72 x 0.28 = 0.2016)
Averaging 500 coins divides that variance by 500. Take the square root and you get the
standard error: 0.020, or 2.0 points.
    (On screen: formula panel line 2: SE = sqrt(0.2016 / 500) = 0.020)
A 95 percent confidence interval is about 1.96 standard errors either side: 68.1 to 75.9
percent. Last week's 70.4 is well inside it.
    (On screen: interval bar under the score, 70.4 marked inside)
And what does 95 percent mean? If you rebuilt the benchmark many times and drew an
interval each time, 95 of every 100 intervals would contain the true accuracy.
    (On screen: 20 stacked interval bars around a dashed 'true p' line; one misses and
    turns red)
Notice the square root. To halve the error bar you need four times as many questions.
Two thousand questions would bring it down to about one point.
    (On screen: interval bar shrinks to half as n goes 500 -> 2000)

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use this for any single pass rate with a few hundred questions or more and a score away
from zero or a hundred.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'one pass rate, n in the hundreds, score between ~10% and
    ~90%')
Not for comparing two models; that is chapter three. Not for small n or extreme scores;
that is next.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'comparing two models -> ch 3', 'small n or near 0/100%
    -> ch 2')
When you see a single benchmark score, think: plus or minus 1.96 times the square root
of p-hat times one minus p-hat over n. About two points at 500 questions.
    (On screen: cue card)

[5:24] 2. Small samples and extreme rates (the Wilson interval)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter two. Small samples and extreme rates.
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this on small benchmarks, like AIME with 30 problems; on slices, like one
language with 40 questions; and on rare events, like a refusal rate or a crash rate.
    (On screen: three tiles: 'AIME: 1 / 30', 'Swahili slice: 33 / 40', 'Harmful outputs:
    0 / 100')
A researcher writes: "The new model solved 1 of 30 AIME problems. Put an error bar on
that for the release note."
    (On screen: chat bubble; whiteboard 1 / 30 = 3.3%)

THINK
Pause and think. Try the formula from chapter one. Does the answer make sense?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
Plug it in. p-hat is 1 over 30, 3.3 percent. The standard error is the square root of
0.033 times 0.967 over 30: 3.3 points.
    (On screen: formula panel: SE = sqrt(0.033 x 0.967 / 30) = 0.033)
So the interval is 3.3 plus or minus 6.4: from minus 3.1 percent to 9.8 percent.
    (On screen: interval bar drawn on a 0 to 20% axis; left end pokes below 0 and turns
    red)
Minus three percent accuracy. That is impossible. Something in the method is broken.

OBSERVATION
Why did it break? The formula draws a symmetric bell around p-hat.
    (On screen: symmetric bell centred on 3.3%, left tail crossing 0)
Now look at what really happens. Take a model whose true rate is 3.3 percent and rerun
the 30 problems many times.
Most runs score zero, one or two. Nothing can go below zero, and a few runs score four
or five. The real shape is lopsided, squashed against the wall.
    (On screen: bar chart of Binomial(30, 0.033): P(0)=0.36, P(1)=0.37, P(2)=0.19,
    P(3)=0.06, P(4)=0.01)
A symmetric bell is a fine approximation when there are many questions and the score is
in the middle. Near zero or a hundred, or with few questions, it is wrong.

TOOL
The Wilson interval asks the question the other way round. Which true rates would make 1
out of 30 an unsurprising result?
Slide a candidate true rate along the axis. At 0.6 percent, 1 out of 30 is just
plausible. At 16.7 percent, it is just plausible again. Beyond those, it is too
surprising.
    (On screen: a slider sweeps p from 0 to 25%; the binomial bars for each p update;
    the region where 1/30 is plausible fills green)
So the Wilson interval for 1 of 30 is 0.6 to 16.7 percent. It stays inside zero to a
hundred, and it is honestly wide.
    (On screen: Wilson bar replaces the broken one)
For our 72 percent on 500 questions, Wilson gives 67.9 to 75.8, almost the same as
before. When the simple interval is fine, Wilson agrees with it.
That makes it a safe default. In Python it is one call: proportion confint, with method
equals wilson.
    (On screen: code panel: proportion_confint(1, 30, method='wilson')  # (0.006,
    0.167))
One special case to remember. Zero failures in 100 runs does not mean never. Wilson's
upper bound is 3.7 percent, and the rule of three says about 3 over n.
    (On screen: tile 'Harmful outputs: 0 / 100' gains the bar 0 to 3.7%)

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use Wilson for one pass rate when n is small, or the rate is near zero or a hundred:
small benchmarks, slices, rare failures, saturated scores. Or simply always.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'small n', 'slices', 'rare events', 'scores near 0 or
    100%')
Not for averages of scores like one to ten; use the bootstrap in chapter four. If you
need a guaranteed 95 percent, as in a safety sign-off, use Clopper-Pearson, which is a
little wider.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'non-0/1 metrics -> ch 4', 'guaranteed coverage ->
    Clopper-Pearson')
When you see a small n or a rate near the edge, think: Wilson, never plus or minus 1.96
standard errors.
    (On screen: cue card)

[9:49] 3. Two models, same questions (pairing, McNemar and the p-value)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter three. Comparing two models on the same questions.
    (On screen: chapter card)
This is the most common question on an eval platform, and it comes in many costumes.
Did this week's checkpoint beat last week's? Did the new prompt template help? Did the
vLLM upgrade change the scores? Does the new answer extractor grade differently?
    (On screen: four tiles appear one by one: 'checkpoint vs checkpoint', 'old vs new
    prompt', 'vLLM 0.9 vs 0.10', 'extractor v1 vs v2')
In every one, two versions answer the same questions, and each answer is right or wrong.
    (On screen: a banner across the tiles: 'same questions, right or wrong')
Here is one. The researcher pushes back: "Model A got 360 of 500 right, model B got 375,
same 500 questions. Three points. Surely that's real?"
    (On screen: whiteboard: A 360/500 = 72%, B 375/500 = 75%)

THINK
Pause and think. How would you decide whether three points is real? Is there anything
about this setup you could use?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting approach: put an error bar on each model and compare. Each has a standard
error of about two points.
    (On screen: two intervals side by side, overlapping)
Combine them and the difference has a standard error of 2.8 points. Three points is
barely more than one standard error. The p-value is 0.28.
Verdict: unremarkable. But this analysis treats the two models as if they had answered
different questions. They didn't.

OBSERVATION
Line the two models up question by question. Each square is one question.
    (On screen: 25 x 20 grid of grey squares (pilot data items))
Colour each one. Both right. Both wrong. A right and B wrong. A wrong and B right.
    (On screen: squares colour by category: both=light grey, neither=dark grey,
    A_only=red, B_only=navy)
Now the key question. Which of these squares tell you anything about whether B is better
than A?
A question both got right is easy for both. A question both got wrong is hard for both.
They add to both scores equally and cancel in the difference.
    (On screen: the 320 both-right and 85 both-wrong squares fade out)
All of the evidence lives in the 95 questions where they disagree: 40 where A won, 55
where B won.
    (On screen: remaining red and navy squares slide into two piles: 40 and 55)
The unpaired test was charging the comparison for question difficulty, the biggest
source of noise, which cancels out entirely when you pair.

TOOL
So ask a simple question. If A and B were equally good, each disagreement would be a
fair coin flip.
Flip 95 fair coins. How often do you get a split as lopsided as 55 to 40?
    (On screen: 95 coin flips animate, tallies for A and B)
Here is the distribution of B's wins out of 95, if the coin is fair. Our 55 sits out
here.
    (On screen: bar chart Binomial(95, 0.5), shade tails at 55 and above and 40 and
    below)
The shaded tails add up to 0.15. That is McNemar's test: a binomial test on the
disagreements. A split this lopsided happens about 15 percent of the time by chance.
    (On screen: code panel: binomtest(40, 95, 0.5).pvalue  # 0.15)
Compare that with 0.28 from the unpaired test on the same data. Pairing shrank the error
bar on the difference from 2.8 points to 1.95, for free.
Better still, report the paired interval on the difference: 3 points, from minus 0.8 to
plus 6.8. It says the gap could be zero, or as large as seven.
    (On screen: interval bar for the difference, crossing 0)
Run the same comparison on 2,000 questions with the same proportions, and McNemar gives
0.002. Same models, same gap. Significance measures your evidence, not the size of the
effect.
Now, careful with the word p. We have met three different things called p. p-hat is the
score. 95 percent is the confidence level you chose. And the p-value comes out of a
test.
    (On screen: three boxes: 'p-hat: the score (0.72)', '95%: confidence level',
    'p-value: from a test (0.15)')
The p-value is the chance of data at least this extreme if there were no difference. It
is not the chance that there is no difference.
    (On screen: two statements, the wrong one crossed out)

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use McNemar whenever two versions answer the same questions with right or wrong answers:
checkpoints, prompts, infrastructure, graders.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: the four tiles from the start, ticked)
Not when the questions differ, such as two benchmark versions: use an unpaired two-
proportion test. Not when scores are one to ten: use a paired bootstrap or paired
t-test.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'different questions -> unpaired test (ch 10)',
    'continuous scores -> paired bootstrap / t-test')
And not for three or more models at once: compare pairs, then correct for multiple
comparisons, as in chapter seven.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: '3+ models -> pairwise + Holm (ch 7)')
When you see two models on the same questions, think: pair them, and count only the
disagreements. That only works if your platform stores per-question results.
    (On screen: cue card)

[15:25] 4. Metrics without a formula (the bootstrap, and grouped questions)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter four. Metrics without a formula.
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this whenever the score is not a simple pass rate: an LLM judge's average from
one to ten, an F1 score, a median latency, a weighted composite of several benchmarks.
    (On screen: tiles: 'Judge mean 6.63', 'F1 0.71', 'p95 latency 4.2 s', 'Composite
    63.4')
A team writes: "Our LLM judge scores 200 answers from 1 to 10. The average is 6.63. Can
you put an error bar on it?"
    (On screen: dot strip of 200 judge scores (pilot data), mean line)

THINK
Pause and think. The coin-flip formula was for right or wrong. What do you do when the
metric is something else?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
One tempting answer: run the judge again on the same 200 answers and see how much the
average moves.
But that only measures the judge's own randomness. It says nothing about how much the
score would change with a different 200 questions, which is usually the bigger effect.
Another tempting answer: look for the right formula. For an average there is one. For a
median, a weighted composite, or a ratio of two metrics, often there isn't.

OBSERVATION
What we would love is to rerun the whole experiment many times with fresh questions,
like the two thousand reruns in chapter one. We can't. We only have one sample.
So here is the trick. Pretend our 200 answers are the whole population. Then a fresh
sample is just 200 draws from them.
With replacement, so some answers get picked twice and some not at all.
    (On screen: draw 200 with replacement; highlight duplicates; caption '73 answers
    left out this time')

TOOL
Compute the average on that resample. Here it is: 6.53.
    (On screen: first resample mean 6.53)
Do it again. And again. Ten thousand times.
    (On screen: fast-forward: histogram of bootstrap means builds up)
The middle 95 percent of those ten thousand averages runs from 6.37 to 6.88. That is
your interval. No formula needed.
    (On screen: shade 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles)
It is five lines of code, and it works for any metric: you only need the per-item
results and the function that turns them into a score.
    (On screen: code panel: for _ in range(10000): idx = rng.integers(0, n, n);
    stats.append(metric(x[idx])))
To compare two systems on the same items, resample the items once and recompute both
metrics on the same resample. That is the paired bootstrap.
One trap. Resample whatever was sampled independently. If questions come in groups, say
five questions per reading passage, resample passages, not questions.
In our example with 40 passages, resampling single questions gives an error bar of 3.3
points. Resampling passages gives 4.3. The naive version was overconfident.
    (On screen: two intervals, the passage one wider)

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use the bootstrap for any metric that is not a single pass rate, for paired comparisons
of such metrics, and whenever items come in groups: passages, repositories, agent
environments.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'any metric', 'paired: same resample for both', 'groups:
    resample the groups')
Not for very small samples, say under 20 items, where it is overconfident; and not as a
fix for a biased metric. It measures noise, not bias.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'n < ~20', 'bias in the metric')
When you see a metric with no formula, think: bootstrap. Resample the independent units
ten thousand times and take the middle 95 percent.
    (On screen: cue card)

[19:25] 5. Can this benchmark see the change? (power and sample size)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter five. Can this benchmark even see the change?
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this before an experiment, not after: when a team picks which benchmarks gate a
decision, or asks for a new eval set to be built.
A researcher writes: "Our new data mix should add about 2 points on reasoning. We'll
compare checkpoints on our 500-question set. Good plan?"
    (On screen: chat bubble; whiteboard: expected gain 2 points, n = 500, same
    questions)

THINK
Pause and think. If the new mix really adds 2 points, how likely is this experiment to
notice?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting answer: run it and look at the p-value. If it is below 0.05, the mix
helped. If not, it didn't.
But "not significant" from a weak experiment is not evidence of no effect. It may just
mean the test could never have seen it.

OBSERVATION
So simulate the plan before running it. Suppose the mix really does add 2 points. Run
the comparison a thousand times on fresh 500-question sets.
Assume, as is typical, that the two checkpoints disagree on about 20 percent of
questions. Then the paired standard error of the difference is the square root of 0.2
over 500: 2 points.
    (On screen: formula panel: SE_diff = sqrt(r / n) = sqrt(0.2 / 500) = 0.020)
A true gain of 2 points is only one standard error. Watch the thousand experiments.
    (On screen: 1000 dots fall into a histogram of estimated gains centred at +2, with
    the significance line at +3.9)
Only about 17 percent of them clear the significance bar. Five times out of six, a real
improvement would be reported as no effect.
    (On screen: the 17% to the right of the line turn green, the rest grey; label 'power
    = 17%')

TOOL
The number to know is the minimum detectable effect: the smallest true gain your
experiment catches 80 percent of the time.
It is about 2.8 standard errors of the difference. Here, 2.8 times 2 points: 5.6 points.
This benchmark cannot reliably see anything smaller.
    (On screen: formula panel: MDE = 2.8 x SE_diff = 5.6 points)
Want to detect 2 points? Solve for n. With 20 percent disagreement, you need about 4,000
questions. Unpaired, you would need about 8,000.
    (On screen: line chart: MDE against n, paired and unpaired curves; markers at n =
    500 (5.6) and n = 4000 (2.0))
Three cheaper levers. Pair the comparison. Use continuous metrics, like the log-
likelihood of the right answer, which are far less noisy than right or wrong. And
average over several nearby checkpoints.

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use this before choosing which benchmarks gate a decision, when sizing a new eval set,
and when anyone says "no significant difference".
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'before the experiment', 'sizing an eval set', 'reading a
    null result')
A small benchmark is still fine for trend-spotting across many checkpoints. Just don't
let it decide a two-point question.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'small sets deciding small gains')
When you see a planned comparison, think: minimum detectable effect is 2.8 standard
errors of the difference. If the expected gain is smaller, get more questions or a
better metric.
    (On screen: cue card)

[23:03] 6. Sampling many answers (pass@k done right)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter six. Sampling many answers.
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this on code and maths benchmarks, where a model gets several attempts and you
report pass at k: the chance that at least one of k attempts is right.
A researcher writes: "We sampled 20 answers per problem. For pass at 5, can't we just
take the average pass rate p and compute one minus one minus p to the fifth?"
    (On screen: chat bubble; formula 1 - (1 - p)^5 on the whiteboard)

THINK
Pause and think. Is there anything wrong with that formula when you apply it to a whole
benchmark?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
Try it on a tiny benchmark of two problems. Problem one is always solved. Problem two is
never solved.
    (On screen: two cards: P1 20/20 green, P2 0/20 red)
The average pass rate is 50 percent. The formula says pass at 5 is one minus 0.5 to the
fifth: 97 percent.
    (On screen: formula evaluates to 0.97)
But the true pass at 5 is 50 percent. Five attempts at problem two still fail every
time.

OBSERVATION
The formula assumes every problem has the same difficulty. Real benchmarks mix problems
the model always solves, sometimes solves, and never solves.
Extra attempts only help on the sometimes problems. So pass at k must be computed per
problem, then averaged.
    (On screen: the two cards each get their own pass@5: 1.0 and 0.0, averaged to 0.5)

TOOL
For each problem, take n samples, say 20, and count c correct, say 3.
    (On screen: a row of 20 samples, 3 green)
Pass at k is the chance that a random k of the 20 include at least one correct one. That
is one minus the number of all-wrong subsets over all subsets: one minus 17 choose 5
over 20 choose 5.
    (On screen: formula panel: pass@k = 1 - C(n-c, k) / C(n, k))
That gives 0.60 for pass at 5, and 0.15 for pass at 1. Do this for every problem and
average.
    (On screen: formula panel: pass@1 = 0.15, pass@5 = 0.60)
This estimator is unbiased, and it uses all 20 samples, not just the first five. And
always ask which metric people mean: pass at k, the average over k samples, or majority
vote over k.
    (On screen: three labels: 'pass@k: any of k right', 'avg@k: mean over k', 'maj@k:
    majority vote')

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use the per-problem estimator whenever a model gets several attempts. Sample n well
above k, so the estimate is stable.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'several attempts per problem', 'n >> k')
Never apply one minus one minus p to the k across a benchmark.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: '1 - (1 - p)^k on a benchmark average')
When you see pass at k, think: per problem, one minus n minus c choose k over n choose
k, then average.
    (On screen: cue card)

[26:23] 7. Many benchmarks, many checkpoints (multiple comparisons)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter seven. Many benchmarks, many checkpoints.
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this on every eval dashboard: forty benchmarks per checkpoint, per-language
slices, and regression alerts that fire when any number moves.
Every new checkpoint runs on 40 benchmarks. Each cell lights up when its change versus
the last checkpoint has a p-value under 0.05.
    (On screen: 40-cell grid)
Today's checkpoint: four cells are lit. "Look, we improved on four benchmarks!"
    (On screen: light 4 cells (pilot data row 0))

THINK
Pause and think. How excited should they be?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
Each lit cell passed a significance test at 0.05. So each one, on its own, looks like a
real change.
Here is the twist. In this simulation, nothing changed. Every checkpoint is the same
model. Watch twenty checkpoints go by.
    (On screen: play 20 rows; cells light where p < 0.05)
18 of the 20 checkpoints lit up at least one cell. All of them are false alarms.

OBSERVATION
Why? Each test has a five percent chance of a false alarm. What happens when you run
forty of them?
The chance that all forty stay quiet is 0.95 to the power forty, about 13 percent. So 87
percent of the time, at least one cell lights up by luck.
    (On screen: formula 1 - 0.95^40 = 0.87)
On average you expect two lit cells per checkpoint from pure noise. A researcher looking
for good news will always find some.

TOOL
The simplest fix is Bonferroni: with 40 tests, require each one to pass at 0.05 divided
by 40, about 0.001.
Holm's method is a slightly smarter version. Sort the p-values. Test the smallest at
0.05 over 40, the next at 0.05 over 39, and so on, stopping at the first failure.
    (On screen: sorted p-values with stepping threshold)
Replay the twenty checkpoints with Holm. False alarms drop from 38 lit cells to none.
    (On screen: replay grid with Holm thresholds)
For dashboards, Benjamini-Hochberg is often better still: it controls what fraction of
the lit cells are false, rather than whether any are.
The bigger fix is design: pick a few decision benchmarks in advance, freeze their
configs, and show intervals on every cell instead of bare deltas.

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use Holm when any false alarm is costly, like a release gate. Use Benjamini-Hochberg for
dashboards and exploration, where a few false leads are acceptable.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'release gate -> Holm', 'dashboard -> Benjamini-
    Hochberg')
Not needed for one pre-registered comparison that you decided on in advance.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'one comparison chosen in advance')
When you see many benchmarks or many checkpoints, think: correct for multiple
comparisons before you believe a lit cell.
    (On screen: cue card)

[30:01] 8. Can you trust the judge? (agreement and Cohen's kappa)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter eight. Can you trust the judge?
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this whenever a model grades another model: an LLM judge for helpfulness, a
safety classifier, a rubric grader. Before trusting it, you compare it with human
labels.
A team writes: "Our judge agrees with human raters on 93 percent of 400 answers when
flagging harmful content. Ship it?"
    (On screen: chat bubble; whiteboard: agreement 93%, n = 400)

THINK
Pause and think. Is 93 percent agreement good? What else would you want to see?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting answer: 93 percent is high. Ship it.
But here is a judge that never flags anything. Humans flagged 20 of the 400 answers as
harmful. The do-nothing judge agrees on the other 380: 95 percent.
    (On screen: a second judge labelled 'always says safe' gets 95% agreement)

OBSERVATION
Agreement is inflated by the easy, common class. When 95 percent of answers are safe,
two raters agree a lot by chance alone.
Look at the confusion matrix. Of the 20 answers humans flagged, the judge caught only 6.
It also flagged 14 safe answers.
    (On screen: 2x2 table: both harmful 6, judge only 14, human only 14, both safe 366)
It catches 30 percent of what matters. The 93 percent was mostly the two raters agreeing
that safe answers are safe.
    (On screen: recall bar: 6 / 20 = 30%)

TOOL
Cohen's kappa corrects for chance. First, the agreement you would expect by luck, from
how often each rater says each label.
Here both say harmful 5 percent of the time, so chance agreement is 0.05 squared plus
0.95 squared: 90.5 percent.
    (On screen: formula panel: p_e = 0.05^2 + 0.95^2 = 0.905)
Kappa is the observed agreement above chance, as a fraction of the room above chance:
0.93 minus 0.905, over 1 minus 0.905. That is 0.26: poor.
    (On screen: formula panel: kappa = (0.93 - 0.905) / (1 - 0.905) = 0.26)
For contrast, a helpfulness judge with balanced labels and 80 percent agreement gets a
kappa of 0.56, moderate. Same idea, very different starting points.
    (On screen: second table: 220, 40, 40, 100; kappa = 0.56)
Two more habits. Measure how often humans agree with each other first; that is the
ceiling for any judge. And check the known judge biases: position, length and self-
preference.
    (On screen: three tags: 'position bias', 'length bias', 'self-preference')

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use kappa with the confusion matrix and per-class recall whenever you validate a judge
or a classifier against humans. For more than two raters, use Fleiss' kappa or
Krippendorff's alpha.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'judge vs humans: kappa + confusion matrix', '3+ raters:
    Fleiss / Krippendorff')
Never trust raw agreement alone, especially when one class is rare.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'raw agreement on a rare class')
When you see a judge's agreement rate, think: what would chance give, and what is its
recall on the class that matters?
    (On screen: cue card)

[33:37] 9. From votes to a ranking (Bradley-Terry and Elo)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter nine. From votes to a ranking.
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this in side-by-side evaluations: raters or a judge see two answers and pick
the better one. Arenas and human preference studies work this way.
An internal arena has five models and 2,080 head-to-head votes. A researcher writes:
"Nova has the best win rate, 61 percent. It's our top model."
    (On screen: leaderboard by raw win rate: Nova 61.2%, Atlas 60.0%, Birch 58.0%, Cedar
    44.7%, Dune 32.7%)

THINK
Pause and think. Is the model with the highest win rate the strongest?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting answer: yes. More wins means a better model.
But look at who each model played.
    (On screen: matchup matrix of game counts appears)
Nova played Dune 500 times and Cedar 250 times, the two weakest models. It met Atlas and
Birch only 40 times each, and lost more than it won.
    (On screen: Nova's row highlighted: 500 vs Dune, 250 vs Cedar, 40 vs Atlas (17-23),
    40 vs Birch (17-23))

OBSERVATION
A win rate mixes up how good you are with who you happened to face. Beating weak
opponents is easy.
What we want is a strength for each model that explains every result at once, taking the
opponent into account.

TOOL
The Bradley-Terry model gives each model a strength, and says the chance that i beats j
depends only on the difference in strength.
    (On screen: formula panel: P(i beats j) = 1 / (1 + 10^((R_j - R_i) / 400)))
On the Elo scale, 400 points means 10 to 1 odds. A 60 percent win rate is about 70
points; 55 percent is about 35.
    (On screen: small curve of win probability against rating gap, markers at 35, 70 and
    400)
Fit all 2,080 votes at once. Atlas comes out at 1094, Birch 1082, Nova 1042, Cedar 1000,
and Dune 921. Nova drops to third.
    (On screen: leaderboard re-sorts: Atlas 1094, Birch 1082, Nova 1042, Cedar 1000,
    Dune 921)
Then bootstrap the votes to get error bars. Atlas and Birch overlap, so they are tied
for first. Nova is clearly third.
    (On screen: rating intervals: Atlas 1066-1126, Birch 1052-1114, Nova 1015-1071, Dune
    891-949 (Cedar fixed at 1000); rank ranges 1-2, 1-2, 3, 4, 5)
Report rank ranges, not single ranks, and be wary of a model that was tested more, or
against easier opponents, than the others.

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use Bradley-Terry for any set of pairwise preferences: human side-by-side studies, judge
comparisons, arenas.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'pairwise preferences -> one fitted rating each')
Not raw win rates when opponents differ. And not sequential online Elo updates, which
depend on the order of the games.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'raw win rates', 'order-dependent online Elo')
When you see a leaderboard of win rates, think: who did they play? Fit Bradley-Terry and
show rank ranges.
    (On screen: cue card)

[36:51] 10. Leaked or worn out? (contamination, and comparing different question sets)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SITUATION
Chapter ten. Leaked or worn out?
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this when a score looks too good, when a public benchmark has been online for
years, or when everyone's scores bunch up near the top.
A researcher writes: "Our model gets 92 percent on a famous public maths benchmark. Is
that real skill, or has it seen the test?"
    (On screen: chat bubble; whiteboard: public set 92%)

THINK
Pause and think. How could you tell, without access to the training data?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting answer: search the training data for the test questions. If they are not
there, it's clean.
But exact search misses paraphrases, translations, solutions posted on forums, and
synthetic data generated from the benchmark. Absence of exact matches proves little.

OBSERVATION
Here is a behavioural test instead. Write a fresh set of 1,000 problems in the same
style and difficulty, never published anywhere.
    (On screen: a new stack of problems labelled 'fresh, unpublished')
If the skill is real, the score should barely move. If the model memorised the test, it
should drop.
Model X: 92 percent on the public set, 81 on the fresh one. Model Y: 88 and 87.
    (On screen: two pairs of bars: X 92 vs 81, Y 88 vs 87)

TOOL
Now, these are different questions, so we cannot pair them. This is the unpaired case
from chapter three.
The standard error of the difference is the square root of each set's p times one minus
p over n, added together. For model X, that is 1.5 points.
    (On screen: formula panel: SE = sqrt(0.92 x 0.08 / 1000 + 0.81 x 0.19 / 1000) =
    0.015)
An 11 point drop is more than seven standard errors. That is far beyond noise: strong
evidence of contamination.
For model Y, a 1 point drop is less than one standard error, a p-value of 0.5.
Consistent with real skill.
Other checks: n-gram overlap with training data, whether the model can complete a test
question from its first half, and whether performance drops on problems published after
its training cutoff.
And saturation is the other way benchmarks wear out. When top models all score above 90
percent, the remaining errors are often label mistakes, and the benchmark stops
separating models.

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use a fresh look-alike set, or a set published after the training cutoff, whenever a
public benchmark score matters for a decision.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'fresh look-alike set', 'post-cutoff problems')
Different question sets mean an unpaired test, never McNemar.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'McNemar across different question sets')
When you see a suspiciously high public score, think: rerun on fresh look-alikes and
compare, unpaired.
    (On screen: cue card)

SITUATION
Chapter eleven. Model or pipeline?
    (On screen: chapter card)
You meet this on call. A number moves, and someone must decide whether the model changed
or the evaluation broke.
Monday morning, a researcher writes: "Overnight, 31 of our 40 benchmarks dropped by one
to four points. The run is diverging. Should we roll back?"
    (On screen: dashboard grid with 31 cells red)

THINK
Pause and think. What would you check before rolling back a training run?
    (On screen: countdown card)

NAIVE ANSWER
The tempting answer: many benchmarks fell together, so the model got worse. Roll back to
Friday's checkpoint.
But a broad, simultaneous drop across unrelated benchmarks is exactly what a pipeline
fault looks like too. Rolling back costs days of training.

OBSERVATION
Check things in order, cheapest first. One: completeness. Did every question get an
answer?
    (On screen: checklist item 1 highlighted)
All 500 answered, but the truncation rate jumped from 0.3 percent to 6 percent. Answers
are being cut off.
    (On screen: truncation-rate sparkline jumps from 0.3% to 6%)
Two: lineage. What changed besides the checkpoint? The diff shows the inference engine
was upgraded on Sunday, and its default output length changed.
    (On screen: config diff: engine 0.9.2 -> 0.10.0, max_tokens default 4096 -> 1024)
Three: the reference model. A frozen old checkpoint runs through the same pipeline every
night. If it moves, the pipeline moved.
    (On screen: line chart: reference model flat for weeks, then a step down on Monday)

TOOL
The reference model went from 360 to 345 correct on the same 500 questions. Same model,
same questions, two pipeline versions. That is chapter three.
Pair them. 25 questions went from right to wrong, 10 from wrong to right. McNemar gives
a p-value of 0.017.
    (On screen: two piles: 25 and 10; code panel binomtest(10, 35, 0.5).pvalue  # 0.017)
The pipeline changed the score of a model that did not change. Fix the output length,
rerun, and the training run is fine.
So the triage order is: completeness, lineage, reference model, statistics, and only
then the model.
    (On screen: checklist: completeness, lineage, reference model, statistics, model)
And every incident like this should end in a new automatic check, here an alert on
truncation rate, so it never needs a human again.

FIT: WHEN TO USE IT, WHEN NOT
Use a pinned reference model and health metrics, such as completion, error, truncation
and extraction-failure rates, on every eval run.
    (On screen: fit card, USE: 'pinned reference model', 'health metrics on every run')
Never blame the model before ruling out the pipeline.
    (On screen: fit card, NOT: 'roll back first, check later')
When you see many scores move at once, think: completeness, lineage, reference model,
statistics, then the model.
    (On screen: cue card)

REFERENCE: the formulas behind the video (from the Evaluation Field Guide)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This page is for the night before. Each line is the tool, when to reach for it, and the number from the book's running example so you can check your memory.

Standard error of a pass rate. √(p(1 − p)/n). At 72 percent on 500 questions: 2.0 points. Halving it takes four times the questions.

95 percent interval. p̂ ± 1.96 SE (Wald) is fine for a few hundred items away from 0 or 100 percent: 68.1 to 75.9 percent. Use Wilson by default (67.9 to 75.8 percent), and always near the extremes: 1 of 30 gives 0.6 to 16.7 percent where Wald gives a negative lower bound. Clopper-Pearson when you need guaranteed coverage.

Bootstrap. Resample the independent units with replacement 10,000 times, recompute the metric, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Works for any metric. Resample clusters (passages, repos, environments), not items, when items share them.

Design effect for clustered items. Variance multiplies by 1 + (m − 1)ρ. Five questions per passage at ρ = 0.2: 1.8 times the variance, 1.34 times the error bar.

Comparing two models on the same items. Pair them. McNemar on the discordant counts b and c: exact binomial test of b against b + c at one half. Running example: 40 versus 55 gives p = 0.15, where the unpaired test on the same data gives 0.28. Paired SE of the difference: 1.95 points versus 2.8 unpaired.

Continuous per-item scores. Paired t-test on differences, or paired bootstrap, or permutation test (swap labels within items).

p-value. Probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. Not the probability the null is true. Says nothing about effect size. Lead with the confidence interval on the difference.

Minimum detectable effect. About 2.8 standard errors of the difference (α = 0.05 two-sided, 80 percent power). Paired with discordance rate r: 2.8 √(r/n). At r = 0.2, detecting 2 points needs about 4,000 items; unpaired at 70 percent accuracy, about 8,000.

pass@k. Generate n > k samples, count c correct, per problem 1 − C(n − c, k)/C(n, k), averaged over problems. n = 20, c = 3: pass@1 0.15, pass@5 0.60. Never 1 − (1 − p)^k across a benchmark. Ask whether people mean pass@k, avg@k or maj@k.

Multiple comparisons. Forty null tests at 0.05: 87 percent chance of at least one false positive. Bonferroni α/m; Holm (sorted, α/m, α/(m − 1), …) is uniformly better; Benjamini-Hochberg (largest i with p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ iq/m) controls the false discovery rate and suits dashboards.

Cohen's kappa. (observed − chance)/(1 − chance). 80 percent agreement with 54.4 percent chance gives 0.56, "moderate". Collapses when one class is rare: 92 percent agreement, kappa 0.29. Report the confusion matrix and per-class recall beside it. Measure human-human agreement first; it is the ceiling.

Bradley-Terry / Elo. P(i beats j) = 1/(1 + e^−(sᵢ − sⱼ)). 400 Elo points = 10:1 odds; 60 percent win rate ≈ 70 points; 55 percent ≈ 35. Fit all votes at once, bootstrap intervals, show rank ranges.

Online tests. Randomise by user, analyse at user level, reduce variance with CUPED, do not peek without a sequential method.
