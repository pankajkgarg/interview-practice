Benchmarks wear out: contamination, saturation, efficient evaluation and online tests

Use this whenA benchmark might be leaked or saturated, you want a cheaper subset, or you are measuring with live users (A/B tests).

The previous chapters took the benchmark as given and asked how to read its numbers. This one turns the question around: when is the benchmark itself the problem? Four failure modes come up again and again in conversations with eval teams, and each has a recognisable signature and a standard response. The chapter ends with the other half of evaluation, measuring a model in front of real users, where the statistics are the familiar A/B-testing kind.

Contamination

A benchmark measures generalisation only if the model has not seen the test questions during training. Pretraining corpora are scraped from the web, and popular benchmarks are on the web, often with answers, in GitHub repos, blog posts, and forum threads discussing them. So contamination is the default, not the exception, and its signature is a model that scores conspicuously better on the public test set than on fresh questions of the same kind.

There are three families of defence, and you should be able to name all three. The first is detection in the training data: n-gram overlap between benchmark items and the training corpus (the GPT-3 and Llama papers used 13-gram and similar overlaps), which a training-data team can run if they have the corpus indexed. The second is detection from the model’s behaviour: does the model assign suspiciously high likelihood to the exact benchmark text, can it complete a question verbatim from its first half, does it do better on canonical answer orderings than on shuffled ones, does it reproduce the specific wrong answers in a benchmark’s known label errors? The third, and the most robust, is to avoid the problem by construction: private held-out sets that never touch the internet, canary strings in benchmark files so that well-behaved crawlers can filter them (BIG-bench introduced this), and dynamic benchmarks that keep adding new problems dated after the model’s training cutoff. LiveCodeBench, for example, tags each problem with its contest release date so you can evaluate only on problems published after a model’s cutoff, and a model whose score drops sharply on post-cutoff problems was likely contaminated on the older ones. Scale AI’s GSM1k (May 2024) applied the same idea to grade-school maths: a freshly written, GSM8K-style set exposed accuracy drops of up to 8 points for some model families, and the gap correlated with how likely the model was to generate GSM8K items verbatim.

The platform implications are concrete: store the provenance and publication date of every item, support evaluating on date-filtered subsets, keep internal held-out sets access-controlled like secrets, and make it easy to rerun a benchmark with paraphrased or perturbed items as a contamination check.

Saturation

A benchmark saturates when frontier models score near its ceiling, at which point it stops separating them. MMLU went from a hard benchmark in 2021 to near-saturated by 2024, which drove the creation of MMLU-Pro and GPQA, and the cycle has repeated with every generation since: GSM8K to MATH to AIME and FrontierMath, HumanEval to SWE-bench Verified and beyond. Saturation is partly the statistics of Chapter 7 in disguise. When accuracy approaches 100 percent, the remaining errors are often label errors in the benchmark itself (independent audits have found error rates of several percent in widely used sets), so the last few points measure the benchmark’s noise rather than the model.

The platform response is lifecycle management. A benchmark has a useful life during which it discriminates between the models a team actually trains, and the platform should be able to show that discrimination, for example the spread in scores between the models currently in comparison relative to the run-to-run noise (the signal-to-noise ratio from Chapter 9). When it falls, the benchmark moves from the decision set to the regression-only set, which still catches catastrophic breakage cheaply.

Benchmark design, briefly

Since part of the job is building “problem sets” and “benchmark configurations”, it helps to know what makes a good one, in the words a researcher would use. Validity: does a high score mean what the benchmark’s name claims? A “coding” benchmark of short self-contained functions does not measure the ability to work in a large repository. Reliability: is it big enough and clean enough that reruns agree, which is everything in Chapters 7 to 9. Discriminative power across the models you care about, which is the opposite of saturation. A clear, programmatically checkable grading rule, or a validated judge if it cannot be checked. And documented free choices: prompt format, few-shot exemplars, answer extraction, all frozen per version.

A specific practical warning about answer extraction belongs here because it causes more silent eval bugs than anything else. A generative benchmark is only as good as the code that pulls the final answer out of the model’s text. Reasoning models write long chains of thought, put their final answer in varied formats, and sometimes restate intermediate results that a careless regex picks up. Hugging Face’s Math-Verify comparison found that changing only the extraction and equivalence-checking logic moved a MATH score by five points on identical model outputs (Chapter 3). Treat the extractor as code under test, with a unit-test suite of tricky real outputs.

Efficient evaluation: item response theory

Full benchmark suites are expensive on every checkpoint, so it is natural to ask whether a well-chosen subset gives almost the same answer. Item response theory (IRT), borrowed from educational testing, models each question as having a difficulty and a discrimination (how sharply it separates weaker from stronger models) and each model as having an ability. Fitted on many models’ past results, it lets you pick the questions that are most informative and estimate a model’s full-benchmark score from a small subset. The “tinyBenchmarks” paper (Polo et al., 2024) showed that around 100 well-chosen items can estimate full-benchmark accuracy on several popular benchmarks to within about two points.

The trade-off is that an IRT subset is tuned on past models and may become less accurate on a model that behaves differently from them, which is exactly the model you most want to evaluate accurately. A sensible platform pattern is to use small, IRT-selected subsets for frequent in-training checkpoints, where the goal is spotting trends cheaply, and full sets for checkpoint promotion and release decisions.

Online evaluation: the A/B test

Offline evaluation asks how a model does on a fixed set of questions; online evaluation asks how real users respond to it. For a deployed assistant such as Copilot, the second is the ground truth and the first is a proxy. This section assumes basic A/B testing and only flags what is specific to LLM products.

The randomisation unit matters: randomise by user, not by request, because a user who sees two models in one session contaminates both arms, and then compute standard errors at the user level (the clustering point from Chapter 7 again). Metrics are noisy and delayed: thumbs-up rates are sparse and biased toward unhappy users, so teams lean on behavioural proxies such as regeneration rate, copy rate, conversation length and return visits, each of which can be gamed by a model that is merely more verbose. Variance reduction matters a lot because LLM product metrics have heavy user-level variance; CUPED (Deng et al., Microsoft, 2013), which uses each user’s pre-experiment behaviour as a covariate, typically cuts variance substantially and was developed at Microsoft. Peeking at an A/B test daily and stopping when it crosses p < 0.05 inflates the false positive rate badly; either fix the sample size in advance or use a sequential method designed for continuous monitoring. And interleaving, where results from both models are merged into one response list, is a far more sensitive design for ranking-style outputs, which matters for search and retrieval features.

The deepest point, and a good one for an interview, is the relationship between the two worlds. Offline evals are cheap, fast and reproducible but only correlate with what users experience; online tests measure the real thing but are slow, expensive and cannot be run on every training checkpoint. Mature teams treat offline evals as a filter in front of online tests and, crucially, measure how well each offline metric predicts online outcomes over time, retiring offline metrics that stop predicting.

What to take from this chapter

Benchmarks are consumables. Contamination is the default for anything public; defend with training-data overlap checks, behavioural probes, canary strings, private held-out sets and date-filtered dynamic benchmarks, and store item provenance so the platform can do all of these. Saturation turns the last few points of a score into label noise; track each benchmark’s discriminative power and retire it to a regression-only role when that falls. The answer extractor is code under test. Item response theory can cut eval cost by an order of magnitude for trend-spotting, with full sets kept for decisions. Online A/B tests for LLM products need user-level randomisation, variance reduction such as CUPED, and discipline about peeking, and the best teams continually measure how well offline numbers predict online ones.
